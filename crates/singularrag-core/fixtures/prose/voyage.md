# The Aurelia voyage

In the summer of 1893 the schooner *Aurelia* left the far north of Norway on a short scientific cruise into the Greenland Sea. This account follows the ship's log and the letters of the people on board. The voyage was paid for by the Bergen Museum, which wanted specimens and soundings from waters that few of its collectors had ever seen.

## Departure

The *Aurelia* sailed from the port of Tromsø on the second of August with a crew of eleven. Her master was captain Ingrid Halvorsen, who had run sealing trips out of the same harbour for nearly a decade and knew the ice better than most men twice her age. The naturalist on board was Tomas Berg, a quiet curator from the Bergen Museum who had never been further north than Trondheim. He brought two cases of jars, a dredge, a thermometer that could be lowered on a line, and a notebook he filled from the first morning.

The first week was calm. Berg sorted plankton on the deck while the cook complained about the smell. Halvorsen kept the ship on a westerly course and took a noon sight every day the sun allowed. The crew took bets on how long the naturalist would last before he was sick over the rail; the winner collected on the fourth day.

## The storm

On 14 August the barometer fell faster than anyone aboard had seen. By the afternoon a gale was blowing out of the north-east, and by nightfall the sea was running higher than the foremast yard. Halvorsen ordered the topsails furled and the hatches battened, and she stood at the wheel herself for most of the night.

A wave carried away the port boat and half of the deck cargo. Two of Berg's specimen cases went over the side with it, and he spent the dark hours wedged in the saloon holding the survivors on his knees. Nobody was lost. When the wind eased at dawn the *Aurelia* had been driven more than forty miles off her course, her mainsail was in rags, and the crew had not slept in a day and a half. Halvorsen wrote in the log that it was the worst blow of her career and that the ship had behaved better than she deserved.

## Kolbeinsey

Two days after the gale the lookout sighted Kolbeinsey, a low rock north of Iceland that is barely more than a skerry. Halvorsen anchored in its lee to repair the sail, and Berg was rowed ashore with a sailor to help him. The islet had no soil and no fresh water, only bare basalt, a crust of lichen and a great many birds.

Berg stayed on the rock for six hours. He collected lichens, eggs from a colony of kittiwakes and a bucket of the small crustaceans that lived in the pools. He also measured the islet by pacing it, and wrote that it was smaller than the charts claimed and that the sea was plainly eating it away.

## The report

The *Aurelia* returned to Tromsø on the second of September. Over the winter Tomas Berg wrote up the cruise and delivered his report to the Bergen Museum in the spring of 1894. It described the specimens that survived, the temperature soundings, and the shrinking of Kolbeinsey, and it gave captain Halvorsen credit for bringing the ship home through the storm. The Museum printed the report in its yearbook and bought the remaining jars for its collection. It never funded a second voyage.
